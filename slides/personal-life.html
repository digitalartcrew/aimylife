Why It Felt Useless Last Time

The tool is fine. The prompt was empty.

Almost every disappointing AI answer is missing the same three things.

No context

It does not know your situation, your budget, your kids' ages, or your deadline — unless you say so.

No role

"Help me with my budget" gets a generic answer. "Act as a frugal household planner" gets a useful one.

No format

You wanted a shopping list. You got five paragraphs. Ask for the shape you actually want.

Session 1 • Setup

Pick one. The free tier is enough.

Everything in this track works on a free account. Do not let tool comparison become the thing that stops you.

  • 1 Put it on your phone first. The habit forms on the device you already hold forty times a day, not on a laptop you open twice.
  • 2 Turn on voice input. Most people will not type a paragraph of context. Nearly everyone will say it out loud.
  • 3 Pin it to your home screen. If it takes three taps to find, you will not use it.
The Core Skill

Context · Role · Task · Format

Four lines. It works for almost everything you will ask.

Context: I have two kids, a $180/week grocery budget, and about 30 minutes to cook on weeknights. Role: Act as a practical meal planner who cooks on a budget. Task: Plan five weeknight dinners for us. Format: A table of meals, then one combined shopping list grouped by aisle.
You do not have to label the lines. The labels are a training wheel — keep them until the four pieces come out of your mouth automatically.
Session 1 • Daily Wins

Three things to try this week

Decode the letter

Insurance, school, landlord, tax.

"Explain this letter in plain English. What is it asking me to do, and by when?"

Draft the hard email

The one sitting in your drafts.

"Rewrite this to be firm but not rude. Keep it under 120 words."

Think out loud

For decisions you keep circling.

"Ask me five questions that would help me decide, then tell me what you would weigh most."
Session 2 • Household Admin

Stop re-asking. Start reusing.

One-off questions are useful. Templates are where the time actually comes back. The jobs worth templating are the ones that return every single week.

Comes back weekly

  • Meal planning and the shopping list
  • The budget check-in
  • Calendar and appointment juggling

Comes back seasonally

  • School forms and permission slips
  • Renewals, insurance, taxes
  • Birthdays, travel, holidays
The Template Habit

Write it once, bracket the rest

When a prompt works, do not lose it. Replace the specifics with brackets and save it.

Weekly budget check-in

Act as a blunt but kind household budget coach. This week we spent: [paste the numbers] Our monthly target is: [amount] Our non-negotiables are: [rent, childcare, medication] Tell me: where did we drift, what is one change worth making, and what should I ignore because it is noise?
Keep a note on your phone called Prompts. Five saved templates beat five hundred one-off conversations.
Making It Stick

The fifteen-minute weekly reset

Same time every week. Four prompts. Then close the laptop.

  • 1 Look back. "Here is what happened this week. What did I say I would do and did not?"
  • 2 Clear the inbox. "Draft replies to these four emails. Short, friendly, no filler."
  • 3 Plan the food. Run your saved meal template.
  • 4 Look ahead. "Here is next week's calendar. What am I going to be unprepared for?"
Be Careful Here

What not to hand over

Do not outsource the decision

Medical, legal, financial, immigration. Use it to understand your options and write down your questions — then take those questions to a human who is accountable for the answer.

Do not paste other people's data

Your child's school records, a friend's medical details, anyone's ID numbers. If it is not yours to share, do not put it in the box.

It will state wrong things in a confident voice. Dates, dosages, amounts, deadlines, names — check anything you would be upset to get wrong.
